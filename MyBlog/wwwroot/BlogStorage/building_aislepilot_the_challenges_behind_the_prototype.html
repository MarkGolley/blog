Building AislePilot: The Challenges Behind the Prototype

I recently built AislePilot, a planning tool that generates weekly meals, builds a supermarket-aware shopping list, and keeps everything tied to a household budget. On paper it sounded quite straightforward. In practice, it quickly became a reliability and UX problem as much as an AI problem.

This post is a breakdown of the real challenges I hit while building it, and what I changed to make it usable rather than just impressive in a demo.

What AislePilot was trying to solve

The aim was simple enough:

  • Generate a realistic weekly plan based on dietary modes, household size, and cook days
  • Produce a shopping list grouped by supermarket aisle order
  • Keep costs visible so users can compare plan total vs weekly budget
  • Export results to formats that are useful in real life (PDF and checklist text)

The difficulty is that each one of those needs to stay consistent as users keep changing inputs, swapping meals, rebalancing leftovers, and regenerating the plan.

Challenge 1: AI plans are great until they are not

The biggest early lesson was this: AI output quality varies, and even when quality is good the call itself can still fail. Timeouts, transient API errors, odd payload formatting, and missing keys all happen in normal operation.

So I treated AI as a preferred path, not the only path. AislePilot now has layered behaviour:

  • Try AI generation first when configured
  • Retry transient failures (429/5xx style responses) with short backoff
  • Sanitise and validate AI payloads before they can shape meal output
  • Fallback to curated local meal templates when AI is unavailable

That fallback changed everything. It means users still get a useful plan even when the model is down or unconfigured, and it keeps the experience stable in dev/test environments.

Challenge 2: validation became part of product design

I started with standard form validation and quickly realised it was not enough. For this app, validation is effectively product logic.

Some examples:

  • Supermarket must be one of the supported options
  • Dietary modes must be recognised and at least one must be selected
  • Custom aisle order needs enough departments to be meaningful
  • Constraint combinations that match no meals should fail fast with clear feedback

Without this, users submit valid-looking forms but get incoherent results. Adding defensive validation made the UI feel more predictable and reduced strange edge-case plans.

Challenge 3: “swap meal” sounds easy, state management says otherwise

Swapping one meal for another sounds trivial until you want it to feel good repeatedly. The main issue was avoiding repetitive swaps while preserving constraints and keeping budget/reasoning coherent.

I ended up carrying swap history state per day so previously seen meals are tracked and not immediately repeated. This was one of those hidden complexity features: tiny UI action, surprisingly deep state handling.

Optional deep dive Why leftovers and swaps were the trickiest interaction model.

Leftovers are generated from cook-day allocations, not as an isolated toggle.

When users rebalance leftover days, the request needs to retain the full setup context while changing only leftover source day indexes.

On top of that, meal swapping needs to avoid cycling the same meals back in for a specific day. So the app serialises day-by-day swap history and reuses it on each swap request.

The end result is much better UX, but it required treating "simple controls" like a small state machine.

Challenge 4: keeping the flow safe under repeated POST actions

AislePilot does a lot through forms: generate, suggest-from-pantry, swap, rebalance, export. Repeated POST interactions meant I needed to be strict about safety and abuse control from the start.

  • Anti-forgery validation across all write-like actions
  • Rate limiting policy on write endpoints
  • Return URL hardening (local-only and no recursive loop back into itself)

None of this is flashy, but these protections stopped a lot of future pain and made the app safer to expose publicly.

Challenge 5: export quality had to match the planner quality

I did not want exports to feel like an afterthought, so I spent longer than expected on the document layout and structure. The PDF includes overview, aisle-grouped shopping sections, and full recipe steps with practical formatting.

The checklist export is deliberately simpler and phone-friendly, because in a supermarket you often just need quick tick-box lines and aisle grouping, not a polished report.

This was another product lesson: “usable at home” and “usable in-store” are two different contexts and often need two different output formats.

Challenge 6: pantry suggestions needed to avoid fake confidence

The pantry feature suggests meals from ingredients users already have. Initially it over-suggested by matching generic tokens too loosely. That looked smart but was actually misleading.

I tightened this by:

  • Normalising pantry tokens and ingredient aliases
  • Separating generic pantry items from specific ingredient intent
  • Only returning fully matched suggestions for “can make now”

This made results stricter, but much more trustworthy.

What I learned building it

  • Reliable fallback paths matter more than flashy happy-path demos
  • Validation logic is part of product quality, not just backend hygiene
  • Small UX interactions can hide very non-trivial state management
  • Security basics (anti-forgery, rate limits, safe redirects) should be built in early
  • Export design is a real feature when users act on output in the real world

AislePilot started as “generate me a meal plan” and ended up being a proper systems exercise across AI integration, guardrails, UX continuity, and operational reliability. It is still a prototype, but it now behaves like something I would trust people to actually use.
